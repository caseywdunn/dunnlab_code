# Writing style

Read this before drafting or revising any manuscript prose, including the supplement, captions, and responses to reviewers.

A paper tells a story about biology. Readers come to learn what question was asked, what was found, why it matters, and how far to trust it. Methods and numbers support that story; they are not the story. Agent-drafted prose tends to invert this. It reports what was done, in dense and over-specified sentences, and leaves the reader to work out why. Write for a biologist who does not know the project and is reading because of the organisms and the question. It is the writer's job to make the reader's job easy.

## Find the story first

Develop the story from the bottom up, from data to understanding, and tell it from the top down, from understanding to data. Before drafting, be able to answer:

- **What is the problem,** and why does it matter beyond this study?
- **What is the specific question,** the gap in knowledge this work fills? State it as a question about biology. "To learn X, we did Y" and "we sequenced Z" are activities, not questions.
- **What is the answer,** in one or two sentences? This is the take-home message: the paper's central contribution, which the title should state.
- **What does the answer change** in how we understand the problem?

A paper that only presents results, however rigorous, offers information rather than new knowledge. Editors and readers want the new knowledge. Find what is novel or unexpected and make it prominent.

## Story structure

Scientific papers follow the **Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution** arc, whatever the section headings:

- **Opening** (start of the Introduction): the larger problem, the organisms or system, and why a reader should care. Open wide enough to reach the intended audience, then narrow to your area.
- **Challenge** (end of the Introduction): the specific question or gap. An introduction is not a literature review; it identifies the boundary of what is known and shows where the hole is.
- **Action** (Methods and Results): what you did and what you found, in the order that best answers the question, which is not necessarily the order the work was done.
- **Resolution** (Discussion): what it all means. Synopsize the key results, synthesize them into an answer, and show how that answer advances the problem posed in the Opening.

The Resolution must match the Opening in breadth. If the Opening promises more than the Resolution delivers, the paper overpromises; if the Resolution is broader than the Opening, the paper undersells itself.

Within this arc:

- **Abstract:** the whole story in miniature: the problem and gap, the approach and main findings, and the significance.
- **Methods:** give a brief overview of each method before its details.
- **Results:** lead with the finding, then the supporting evidence. Statistics support the story; they are not the story. Write "Expression was higher in tentacles than in other tissues (Fig. 2)", not "Figure 2 shows the expression in each tissue". Headings and figure titles state findings rather than topics. Cut results that do not serve the story, however much work they took.
- **Discussion:** build to the resolution. This is where the work's meaning is argued; do not simply restate the Results.

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Raise limitations once, where they belong: limits of the methods in the Methods, limits of interpretation in the Discussion. Address them plainly and early in that discussion, and do not let them undermine what the data do show.
- State confidence once and precisely. Do not hedge every sentence, and do not end the paper on uncertainty; the Resolution is the strongest and most memorable part.
- Many apparent limitations come from a mismatch between the question promised in the Introduction and what the methods can answer. Fix the question rather than piling caveats on the answer.

## Paragraphs

- **One point per paragraph.** If you cannot state the point in one sentence, restructure the paragraph rather than polishing it.
- **Point first, usually.** Most paragraphs open with a topic sentence that states the point, then develop it. Put the point last only at important transitions, where building to a conclusion carries the reader, and then give the paragraph a strong opening and ending.
- **Context, content, conclusion.** The first sentence tells the reader what the paragraph is about; the last tells them what to take from it.
- **Link paragraphs.** Each should follow from the one before, so the reader never wonders why they are reading it.
- **Say each thing once, in one place.** Cover a topic fully where it belongs and do not return to it. Do not restate scope, sample sizes, or caveats in each section.

## Sentences

- **Make the characters the subjects and their actions the verbs.** The subject should be what the sentence is about: an organism, a gene, a process, or the authors. "We sequenced", "colonies drift", "the gene duplicated".
- **Put a short, clear subject first, and its verb right after it.** Do not make the reader hold a long subject in mind while waiting for the verb.
- **Old information first, new information last.** Begin with what links the sentence to the previous one; end with what is new and important. Readers put the emphasis at the end of a sentence, so put the main message there.
- **Long sentences need their main point early,** with the elaboration after it.
- **Vary sentence length.** A run of sentences with the same length and shape is tiring and sounds mechanical.
- **Prefer active voice.** Passive voice weakens the sentence and hides the actor. It is acceptable when the actor is unknown or does not matter, as often in the Methods. Objectivity comes from how you treat your data, not from impersonal prose.

## Words

- **Use strong verbs.** Fuzzy verbs say that something happened but not what: *occur, affect, facilitate, perform, conduct, implement, provide, contribute, enable, support*. Replace them with verbs that say what happened: *increase, inhibit, migrate, duplicate, infect*.
- **Turn nominalizations back into verbs and adjectives.** "Populations differed", not "a difference between populations was observed". "We analyzed", not "an analysis was performed". "Colonies were similar", not "colonies showed similarity".
- **Prefer short, plain words.** *Use* rather than *utilize*, *show* rather than *demonstrate*, *method* rather than *methodology*, *start* rather than *initiate*, *length* rather than *duration*.
- **Cut empty amplifiers:** *very, highly, quite, rather, really, entirely, substantially, dramatically, clearly, certainly*.
- **Avoid noun trains.** Four or more nouns in a row ("tissue sample read quality filter") must be unpacked by the reader.
- **Define terms once,** at first use, for the paper's actual audience. Keep abbreviations to a minimum. Use one term for one thing throughout; switching terms makes readers think there are two things.
- **Prefer concrete to abstract.** Name the organism, the tissue, the gene, the place.

## Common weaknesses in drafts

These are failures of clarity and focus that agent drafts are especially prone to, and that co-authors repeatedly flag. Search for them in every draft before sharing it.

- **Abstract subjects with vague verbs.** A sentence whose subject is an artifact of the work (a study, dataset, analysis, table, step, screen, or tool) and whose verb is a fuzzy present-tense one: "The dataset provides broader sampling", "Supplementary tables accompany the figures", "The first filtering step yields retained reads". Ask what actually happened and who did it, and say that: "We added published data from twelve species to broaden sampling". Often the sentence is housekeeping and can be deleted. Software is a tool, so the authors are the actors: "We predicted genes with Tool A and annotated them with Tool B", not "Tool A identified genes and Tool B assigned functions".
- **Over-explanation.** Defining terms the audience knows; reporting things that were not done ("no statistical tests were applied"); stating limits that no reader would assume away; narrating data handling and bookkeeping in the Results; itemizing every count when the argument needs one or two numbers. Ask what the reader would lose without the sentence.
- **Over-specification.** Carrying exact sub-counts, ranges, exclusions, and special cases into the main text. Put details in a table or the Methods, and give the reader the numbers that make the point.
- **Chains of short factual sentences.** A sequence of separate observations that leaves the reader to assemble the point. Combine them into the claim they support, stated with its reasoning ("Because …, we cannot tell whether …").
- **"X, not Y" contrasts** with something the reader never assumed: "The values are medians, not means." State the positive claim. Keep a contrast only when readers are likely to hold the mistaken view.
- **Repetition across sections.** Each section drafted on its own restates the scope, the caveats, and the sample sizes, and a Results sentence repeats a Methods sentence. A paragraph ends by restating its first sentence. After drafting, read the whole manuscript for repeated claims and keep each in the one place where it does most work.
- **Pipeline vocabulary.** Names of workflow branches, filter stages, rules, file types, and the agent's working shorthand. Describe what was done in terms a biologist outside the project would use. If a phrase would be unclear to someone who has not seen the code, rewrite it.
- **Captions that describe their own role** ("This figure summarizes …; see Methods for details"). A caption says what is shown and what it means.
- **Metadiscourse:** sentences about the paper rather than about the biology ("In this section we describe …", "As noted above …").

## The supplement is not a log

Supplementary text meets the same standard as the main text. It explains additional methods, analyses, and results to a reader. It does not record the order in which the work was done, the checks that were run, or the decisions made along the way. Organize it by topic, state each section's purpose at its start, and cut anything a reader would not need to evaluate or reproduce the work. Development history belongs in the analysis repository.

## Revising

Edit in this order: get the story structure right, then make the ideas clear and concrete, then make them flow, then make the language sound good.

- **Brevity comes from selection, not compression.** Decide what the reader needs and cut the rest; do not squeeze everything into denser sentences. Then delete redundancies, the obvious, unnecessary modifiers, metadiscourse, and verbosity. Every word should add content, clarify meaning, or connect ideas.
- **Read it aloud.** Stumbles mark sentences to rewrite.
- **Read as a newcomer would.** Would a biologist outside the project know why each paragraph is there and what to take from it?
- **Check each unit:** does each paragraph and section have a single clear point, clear links to its neighbours, and a resolution?
- **Search for the common weaknesses above** before handing a draft to co-authors.
- **Keep the numbers generated.** Rewriting for style never means typing a value; keep every `{{< meta >}}` shortcode intact.
