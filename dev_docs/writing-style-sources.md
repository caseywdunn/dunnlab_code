# Sources for the manuscript writing-style guidance

Provenance for `skills/dunnlab-manuscript/references/writing-style.md`. This file is for maintainers. It lives outside the skill so that agents never load it: everything in a skill file, HTML comments included, enters the model's context when the file is read.

When you change the style guidance, update this map.

## Published sources

| Source | What the guidance takes from it |
|--------|--------------------------------|
| Mensh B, Kording K (2017) Ten simple rules for structuring papers. *PLoS Comput Biol* 13: e1005619. [doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619) | One central contribution; write for readers who do not know the work; context-content-conclusion paragraphs; avoid zig-zag (say each thing in one place); section-by-section structure of abstract, introduction, results, and discussion; result statements as headings and figure titles |
| Gopen GD, Swan JA (1990) The science of scientific writing. *American Scientist* 78: 550–558. | Subject and verb close together and early; old information in the topic position, new information in the stress position; the actor whose story it is as the subject; express actions as verbs |
| Schimel J (2012) *Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded.* Oxford University Press. | The backbone of the guidance. Science writing as storytelling; the writer's job is to make the reader's job easy; develop the story bottom up, tell it top down; information versus new knowledge; finding the novel and unexpected (SUCCES, ch. 3). Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution and its mapping onto IMRaD; opening and resolution matched in breadth; introductions are not literature reviews; challenges as questions, not "to learn X, we did Y" (ch. 4–9). Methods overview before detail; Results lead with the finding, stats are not stories, cut darlings; Discussion builds to the resolution, which is no place for uncertainty (ch. 8–9). Point-first paragraphs about 75% of the time, point-last at transitions; single clear point per unit (ch. 10–11). Sentences: short topic, verb right after, message in the stress position; long sentences lead with the point (ch. 12–13). Passive voice, fuzzy verbs, and nominalizations; objectivity comes from treatment of data (ch. 14). Plain Anglo-Saxon words, no noun trains, define terms for the audience (ch. 15). Brevity from selection, not compression; delete redundancies, the obvious, modifiers, metadiscourse, verbosity; empty amplifiers (ch. 16). Edit structure, then clarity, flow, language; read it aloud (ch. 17). Limitations: up front, methods limits in Methods, interpretation limits in Discussion, often a question–method mismatch (ch. 18). Chapter mapping from Amy Burgin's [one-page summary](https://burginlab.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/schimel-in-a-sheet-copy.pdf). |
| Williams JM, Bizup J. *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace.* Pearson (many editions). | Characters as subjects and actions as verbs; turning nominalizations back into verbs; cutting redundancy and metadiscourse |
| Pinker S (2014) *The Sense of Style.* Viking. | The curse of knowledge (defining the wrong things, using internal jargon); preferring concrete to abstract; classic style, in which the writer shows the reader the world rather than describing their own procedure |
| Sword H (2012) *Stylish Academic Writing.* Harvard University Press. | "Zombie nouns" (nominalizations); concrete subjects and active verbs; varying sentence length; avoiding stacks of abstract nouns |
| Strunk W, White EB. *The Elements of Style.* | Omit needless words; positive form; active voice by default |
| King S (2000) *On Writing.* Scribner. | Cut on revision (his rule of thumb is about 10 percent); read for the reader's experience |

Schimel, Strunk & White, King, Silvia (*How to Write a Lot*), and Bolker (*Writing Your Dissertation in Fifteen Minutes a Day*) are the five books recommended in [5 books on writing every scientist should read](https://abrilliantmind.blog/5-books-on-writing-every-scientist-should-read/). Silvia and Bolker are about productivity and process and contribute nothing to the style guidance. The broader search found Gopen & Swan, Mensh & Kording, Schimel, Williams, Pinker, and Sword recommended together on scientific-writing reading lists, for example the [ESA Physiological Ecology Section's writing resources](https://esa.org/physiological/resources/writing-resources/) and [Scientist Sees Squirrel](https://scientistseessquirrel.wordpress.com/2016/12/08/some-other-good-books-on-scientific-writing/).

## Lab experience

The "Patterns that make prose read as agent-written" section comes from co-authors' comments on agent-drafted Dunn Lab manuscripts and supplements (October 2026). They flagged abstract subjects with vague present-tense verbs, over-explanation and over-specification, "X, not Y" statements, repetition across sections, phrasing carried over from the pipeline, and supplements that read like a log of the agent's work. The examples in the guidance are invented; keep specifics from real manuscripts out of the skill.
